        \subsection{Empirical Evidence of the Status-Aesthetic Consumption-Social Interaction Linkage}

                Previous research on the uses of culture to sustain social connections provides some preliminary evidence of this model of the social uses of aesthetic goods through culture-mediated social interaction.  
                
                In a classic study, Kadushin (1966) found that Simmelian ``social circles''--in his particular empirical case ``The Friends and Supporters of Psychotherapy''--in the city were clearly segmented based on the specific forms of cultural knowledge that were favored in each micro-setting.  Most importantly, given our discussion so far, Kadushin found that it was differentiation in {\em cultural tastes}--e.g. concert going, visiting the museum, etc.--and other activities intrisically connected to culture-mediated interaction that serve as the criteria that most clearly demarcated social circles in his sample of respondents.  As Kadushin concluded:
                
                \begin{quotation}
                        There are two circles of Friends because there are two ways of becoming a member of Friends.  One way is through active participation in New York City cultural circles.  An interest in psychotherapy, it appears, is prevalent in such surroundings, although not all culturally active circles are supporters of psychotherapy.  Nevertheless mingling with persons interested in the theater, concerts and art, will often bring one in contact with persons knowledgeable about psychotherapy (1966: 797).
                \end{quotation}
              
                Kadushin's findings are thus consistent with the key role that such seemingly ``objectively trivial'' lifestyle habits and tastes (e.g. in comparison to more widely studied traits such as political or religious ideologies and social values) pieces of shared of culture play in sustaining interpersonal bonds.  For instance, Bainbridge and Stark (1981) find that ``matters of individual taste\ldots tend to be quite salient for personal relationships, while objectively important ideologicaly positions may not be [as important]\ldots"  This research supports the conclusion obtained from a variety of field experiments in social psychology that show that ``those who play together stay together.''  For instance, Werner and Parmelee (1979) showed that similarity in cultural pursuits and activities outweighed similarity in ``social attitudes'' and in ``objective'' socio-demographic characteristics when predicting rankings of interpersonal affinity (e.g. liking one another) in a sample of college students.  They conclude that 
                
                \begin{quotation}
                        [w]hile shared activities probably contribute at all levels of successful relationship, they may be especially important in initial stages\ldots Engaging in activities together is immediately pleasurable and may provide the incentive necessary for continued interaction.  Additionally shared pasttimes may be a useful focus around which to conduct the mutual penetration prcoess: The provide both a contect for grdual exchange of information and a readily available `safe' topic when needed (65-66).
                \end{quotation}
                
                Ostrower (1998:  48) finds that high-status culture does not result in upper class cohesion simply through the commonality that results from shared knowledge and tastes related to high-status culture, but more through its role in activating and facilitating the operation of organizational and social network mechanisms that result in the production of events and meetings, as opportunities to form close contacts in those settings where aesthetic goods are the primary focus of conversational interaction.  
                
                Jeannotte (2003) finds that arts consumption is associated with more intense patterns of involvement in community-based voluntary associations.  Individuals who consume more are also more likely to participate in their community life to a larger extent (46, Table 5), with individuals who attend the highest proportion of events being more than five times more likely to volunteer than individuals who attended the lowest amount.  Jeannote concludes that ``\ldots[t]he evidence so far seems to suggest that cultural participation helps to connect individuals to the social spaces occupied by others" (47).   
                
                Elizabeth Long (2003) in her study of women's reading groups in Houston, Texas, finds a similar dynamic in which cultural knowledge and taste becomes the primary content of social interaction. Consistent with the claim that connects the consumption of aesthetic products and sociability, she finds that reading group members ``tend to press books into service for the meanings that they transmit and the conversations they generate (2003:73, 108, 144-48, italics added)." 
                
                Erickson (1996), in her study of culture consumption and network ties in Canadian security firms, provides evidence that connects cultural knowledge with the probability of interacting with other individuals across status and class lines.  She notes that particular forms of cultural knowledge are more useful depending on whether they help the person reach across positions in social space-which she refers to as               coordination culture"-or whether it can only be used among status equals to reinforce social bonds within positions.  In the setting that she studies, she notes that the culture that is traditionally associated with              high-status"-e.g. fine arts consumption (Bourdieu 1984; Ostrower 1998)-is rejected as               useless" precisely because it cannot be used as coordination culture in the workplace. 
                
                The culture that is valued in this market-centered setting is that which is useful in facilitating interaction among persons of different class backgrounds and consists of either popular culture that has little correlation with class or "field-specific" forms of cultural knowledge that are relevant for members of the local industry. Erickson's (1996: 246) finds that knowledge consistent with Fine's model of social uses of popular culture, "culture useful in coordination is uncorrelated or almost uncorrelated with class, popular in every class, and rich enough to provide enjoyable conversation, but the culture that integrates classes may reinforce other kinds of inequalities." 
                
                Lizardo's (2006) more recent analysis explicitly builds on these lines of theory, connecting culture consumption and sociability in order to propose a ``conversion" model that links culture consumption with the possession of larger, sparser social networks. In this model, cultural knowledge and tastes (cultural capital) is ``converted" into the possession of a wider range of social relationships (social capital) by being mobilized in interaction.  He finds that indeed those individuals who are more active culture consumers have a larger ranger of contacts.  Insofar as social relations are experiential lived as a series of micro-encounters (Collins 1975; DiMaggio 1987), then it stands to reason that those who are more likely to engage in aesthetic consumption are also more likely to spend more of their time in informal interaction that can be characterized as "sociability" and in which this cultural knowledge is featured as the focus of attention.  However, the key mechanism postulated by the conversion model connecting aesthetic consumption and social networks-the theorized large propensity of high aesthetic consumers to engage in culture-mediated social interaction-has yet to be rigorously explored.  
                
                In sum, an extensive body of research has indirectly noted the pervasiveness and importance of culture-mediated interaction this empirical research appears to provide some tantalizing indirect evidence that relationships form {\em mainly} on the bases of cultural knowledge and not based on what were thought to be traditional sources of ``status homophily'' (e.g. attitudinal commonalities).  Furthermore, this research confirms the propostion that the primary ``uses'' of culture is social, as shown by the fact that symbolic goods generate conversation and serve as the primary resource with which individuals build and sustain their social networks.  Nevertheless very few researchers have made the culture-mediated interaction phenomenon the focus of their analysis.  
                
                Thus, we know very little about the distribution of culture-mediated interaction in the population at large--it should be fairly widespread if this line of theory in culture and stratification is on the right track--but it should also (due to its like correlation with culture consumption) should be likely to be stratified according to markers of status and class, as well as segmented by social divisions associated with gender and age.  In the next section I turn to an empirical exploration of the culture-mediated interaction phenomenon with the aim of shedding light of some of these issues.
